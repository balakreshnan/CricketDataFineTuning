Reinforcement learning with a length term: accuracy, answer length and energy

Twelve GRPO runs on one task (T20 cricket rate and milestone questions with exact numeric answers) with nvidia/NVIDIA-Nemotron-3.5-Lightning-30B-A3B-BF16, varying how strongly the reward penalises long answers: length coefficient c = 0, 0.1, 0.4 and 1.0, three seeds each, 60 training steps per run. All results below are on 600 held-out questions with four sampled answers each (2,400 answers per evaluation).

Set-up

Task and model

  • Task ("cricket"): T20 cricket rate and milestone questions with exact numeric answers; 458,702 training questions, 600 held out. Three question types of 200 held-out questions each: run rate, chase rate, milestone.
  • Model: nvidia/NVIDIA-Nemotron-3.5-Lightning-30B-A3B-BF16, a hybrid Mamba-Transformer with mixture of experts.
  • Hardware: Rhea cluster, NVIDIA Vera Rubin GPUs (4 per node, 286,524 MiB each), aarch64.

Training

  • GRPO in NVIDIA NeMo RL with a rank-32 LoRA. Each step: 64 questions × 8 sampled answers. 60 steps per run on 4 nodes (16 GPUs).
  • Generation with vLLM 0.33: temperature 1.0, top-p 0.95, at most 4,096 new tokens.
  • Reward from a NeMo Gym environment: a finished, exactly correct answer scores 1 − c × output_tokens / 4096; anything else scores 0. c is the length coefficient; c = 0 is a plain correct/incorrect reward.

Evaluation (same harness for every result unless marked)

  • NeMo Gym 0.7.0; 600 held-out questions × 4 sampled answers = 2,400 answers; temperature 1.0, top-p 0.95, at most 4,096 output tokens.
  • vLLM 0.31 on one GPU, serving the adapter as a LoRA module, 64 concurrent requests.
  • "Cut off" means the answer reached the 4,096-token limit before finishing.

Energy

  • Energy = mean power of the serving GPU (sampled every 15 s) × duration of the evaluation run.
  • The duration includes NeMo Gym start-up. That matters more for short runs, so the throughput of the tuned models is understated.
  • Idle power with the model loaded is about 490–520 W and is included in the energy figures.

    Where the time goes in a step

    Seconds per phase of each training step, from the training log. Choose a run, then play or drag the slider: the bar shows the selected step, drawn to the same scale as the slowest step of that run, so it shrinks as the step gets faster.

      Share of step time by coefficient

      What the data shows. In steps 2–10 generation takes 71–80 s per step at every coefficient. By steps 51–60 it is 59.6 s at c = 0, 29.2 s at c = 0.1, 32.3 s at c = 0.4 and 9.7 s at c = 1.0. Log-probabilities and the gradient update also get shorter with the answers. The refit does not: it stays between 11.7 and 15.1 s in every coefficient and period (the weight transfer itself is about 1.2 s of that). So in the last ten steps at c = 1.0 the refit (11.8 s, 41% of the step) takes longer than generation (9.7 s, 33%), while at c = 0 generation is still 58% of the step and the refit 14%. Averaged over steps 2–60, generation / refit / policy training take 64% / 14% / 13% of a 96.3 s step at c = 0 and 47% / 26% / 14% of a 47.6 s step at c = 1.0.

      Groups: generation = generation; refit = prepare_for_generation_total; log-probabilities = logprob_inference_prep + policy_and_reference_logprobs; policy training = training_prep + policy_training; checkpointing and other = checkpointing (logged every 5 steps, about 2.0 s when written) + step time not covered by a logged phase. The first step after a start or a resume is slower because of warm-up (mean 196 s against 129 s for the step after it, over 17 job starts). Step 1 is left out of the averages; the first step after a resume is not.

      vLLM engines: busy versus idle

      During the generation phase of each step, the training log records how long the 16 vLLM engines were busy and idle on average, and the largest number of requests in flight on one engine.

      What the data shows. Busy share = active / (active + idle). In steps 2–10 the engines are busy 76% (c = 0), 84% (c = 0.1), 80% (c = 0.4) and 72% (c = 1.0) of the generation phase. In steps 51–60 that is 62%, 47%, 39% and 30%. At c = 1.0 the engines are then active for 2.9 s and idle for 6.6 s per step. So as answers shorten, a growing part of the remaining generation phase is engines waiting, not decoding; generation capacity is not what limits the late steps. The log does not say what the engines wait for. The largest number of requests in flight on one engine averages 37–39 across the four coefficients (512 requests over 16 engines is 32 each if spread evenly).

      Training health

      Loss and generation KL error per step. The KL error is the mismatch between the log-probabilities of the vLLM sampler and of the trainer, a check that the weights pushed to vLLM match the trainer's.

      What the data shows. The generation KL error stayed between 0.0005 and 0.0037 over all 720 steps (median per coefficient 0.0008–0.0011), including the steps after a resume. The highest values late in training are in sweep-c1.0-s3: its mean over steps 51–60 is 0.0026, against 0.0011 over its first ten steps and 0.0008–0.0017 over steps 51–60 in the other eleven runs. The median loss is -0.002 at c = 0, 2.20 at c = 0.1, 1.70 at c = 0.4 and 1.47 at c = 1.0, with single-step maxima of 0.17, 28.49, 134.13 and 29.42. The loss scale differs by orders of magnitude between c = 0 and the runs with a length term; the log does not explain why, so the loss is shown as a per-run signal and not for comparison across coefficients.

Energy includes job start-up and, for resumed runs, the second start-up and the steps repeated after the checkpoint.       Findings

      1. A plain correct/incorrect reward (c = 0) was enough to raise accuracy and shorten answers. Accuracy rose from about 86% to 99.85% (mean of 3 seeds, range 99.79–99.88), and answers were about 42% shorter (about 1,585 → 925 tokens) with no length reward. Cut-offs at the token limit went from about 10% to about 0 (0.01%).
      2. The length term buys tokens, not accuracy. c = 0.1: 99.40% (98.54–99.88) at 257 tokens (155–351); c = 0.4: 97.19% (95.12–99.62) at 124 tokens (90–155); c = 1.0: 98.50% (97.50–99.67) at 74 tokens (67–78). No setting with a length term had a higher mean accuracy than c = 0.
      3. Seed-to-seed spread is larger with a length term. The min–max range of accuracy across seeds was 0.08 points at c = 0, 1.33 at c = 0.1, 4.50 at c = 0.4 and 2.17 at c = 1.0; it grows from 0 to 0.4 and is smaller again at 1.0, though with three seeds the last two cannot be ranked. One seed at c = 0.4 (sweep-c0.4-s2) fell to 86.25% on milestone questions while staying above 99% on the other two types.
      4. Energy per correct answer falls with answer length. Base model: 94.7 mWh per correct answer. Means of three seeds: 54.2 mWh at c = 0, 18.7 at c = 0.1, 12.3 at c = 0.4, 9.6 at c = 1.0.
      5. Training gets faster as answers shorten. Step time drops during every run as answers shorten, and drops sooner and further with a larger coefficient (see the step-time curves and the figures under them).
      6. Late in training, fixed costs dominate the step. Generation falls from 71–80 s per step (steps 2–10) to 9.7 s at c = 1.0 (steps 51–60), while the refit of the vLLM engines stays at 11.7–15.1 s and becomes the largest phase (41% of the step). In the same steps the engines are busy only 30% of the generation phase. Training energy per run falls from 17.5 kWh (c = 0) to 8.7 kWh (c = 1.0), because the run is shorter, not because the GPUs draw less power.
      7. The same recipe worked on three architectures. Same harness: Nemotron (hybrid Mamba-Transformer, mixture of experts) 86.50% → 97.71%, 1,565 → 107 tokens; Qwen3.8-27B (dense) 96.00% → 100.00%, 743 → 119 tokens; Qwen3.6-35B-A3B (mixture of experts) 93.46% → 99.17%, 2,186 → 152 tokens, with its milestone accuracy going down from 99.50% to 98.12%.

      Related negative result

      Speculative decoding (tried on 2026-11-23 on the RLVR-tuned Qwen3.8-27B) was slower than the target model alone: 36 tokens/s alone versus 17–25 tokens/s with a draft model (best 0.70×).

      What was not measured

      No network, CPU, disk or host-memory telemetry was collected for these jobs. The only system data are the nvidia-smi GPU metrics (utilisation, memory, power, temperature), the per-phase timings in the training log and the Ray driver's memory lines. This report therefore has no network metrics, and it cannot say how much of the refit or of the engines' idle time is network transfer.

      Caveats

      • Three seeds per coefficient. Means and min–max ranges are shown; no significance test is claimed.
      • The accuracy ranges of c = 0.4 (95.12–99.62%) and c = 1.0 (97.50–99.67%) overlap, so the order of those two settings is not established.
      • One task. A second task (GSM8K) has been submitted and has no results yet.
      • Energy is one evaluation run per adapter with few power samples (7 to 26 for the tuned sweep runs). Durations include harness start-up.
      • Training reward includes the length term, so it is not comparable across coefficients. Use held-out accuracy for comparisons.
      • The sweep runs were 60 steps; the earlier 100-step run used a different seed (42) and step count, so it is shown for context, not as a fourth seed of c = 0.4.
      • The two Qwen models were trained with a different RL implementation (the project's RLVR script). The training-cost table mixes two scoring scripts and is marked accordingly.

      Positioning versus published work

      Based on a search of paper abstracts on 2026-11-26; the papers themselves were not read in full, so this section is provisional.

      Already published: rewarding correct answers and penalising length in reinforcement learning. Examples found: Short-RL (arXiv 2505.12284), AALC (arXiv 2506.20160), Powered Length Penalty (arXiv 2506.10446), Concise Reasoning via RL (arXiv 2504.05185), LEASH (ACL 2026), DDCA (arXiv 2602.02099). The method used here should not be presented as new.

      What these runs appear to add is empirical:

      • a measured accuracy–tokens–energy curve with seed spread on a hybrid Mamba mixture-of-experts model;
      • the c = 0 control, which separates the effect of the correctness reward from the effect of the length term;
      • results on three architectures with the same evaluation harness;
      • measured GPU power on Vera Rubin;
      • a training-cost comparison with LoRA on distilled traces.
